Season 3 · Final Episode
Joey Waronker
Session Drummer · Beck, R.E.M.,
Thom Yorke, Elliott Smith

Joey Waronker grew up sitting in the drum booth with Steve Gadd and Jeff Porcaro — literally inside the sessions his father was producing. Today he runs a full recording setup out of a converted two-car garage and handles remote sessions entirely on his own. This conversation covers how those early experiences shaped his sound, what he had to unlearn along the way, and how the pandemic finally forced him — like a lot of us — to close the gap between doing it and actually understanding it.

Key Takeaways
What's Worth Remembering From This Conversation
1
Sensory reference precedes understanding — and that's useful, not a shortcut
At 9 years old, Joey absorbed how sessions felt — the microphone positions, the snare tuning by ear, the dampening — before he understood any of it technically. Years later, that stored reference gave him something to aim for, even when he didn't have the vocabulary yet. The lesson: listening carefully and often builds the internal standard you'll eventually train your technical skills to reach.
2
Good instincts without technical knowledge leaves you stuck at the same wall
Joey could hear when something was off in a session. But without knowing why, he couldn't communicate with engineers, couldn't fix it himself, and couldn't get out of his own way. He had musical intelligence. What was missing was the technical framework to act on it. Instinct without knowledge produces frustration, not consistent results.
3
The pandemic exposed what years of working hadn't fixed
Joey had been recording at home for years — and genuinely thought he was doing fine. The pandemic gave him time to actually dig in, and he discovered he didn't understand phase. He describes spending 20 hours on something he can now do in 2. Time alone doesn't teach you. Focused study does. The gap between doing it and understanding it only closes one way.
4
Copying someone's approach gets you started. Understanding why it works makes it yours
Brendan O'Brien showed Joey his go-to 414 compressor setting — "set it like this and it always works." Joey still references it. But O'Brien's own caveat: you'll always have to find your version. Different room, different preamps, different variables. Recipes get you started. Understanding gets you consistent results regardless of what session you're walking into.
5
The people who share freely understand that knowledge isn't a competitive threat
Gadd, Kelner, Gadson — that whole generation gave information away openly. Joey does the same: "I don't really feel like I have secrets in here." What works in his studio won't work exactly the same way in yours anyway. The framework transfers. The specific settings don't. That's why sharing the how — not just the what — is what actually moves the craft forward.
"

I thought I knew what I was doing — but I learned over the pandemic that I had no idea about phase. If I got an okay bass drum sound, that would just be the bass drum sound. Whereas now I'm like — that's not right. That has to be better.

— Joey Waronker
For Home Studio Recording Drummers
Where This Connects to Your Work
Phase isn't optional — it's the foundation
Joey spent years recording without understanding phase. He got passable results by feel. Once he understood it, the whole picture changed. If your recordings are inconsistent and you can't explain why, phase is almost always where the answer lives.
Dynamics are a recording tool, not just a playing choice
The Tonic record sounds massive — not because of brute force, but because Joey played at the right level for that room and those mics. Dynamic control for recording is a learnable skill that directly affects how your tracks translate.
Your limitations don't have to limit the result
Joey built a dead zone, a live area, and a mid-range space in a two-car garage. The room you have can work if you know how to work with it. The knowledge gap matters more than the square footage.
Better gear only helps once you know what to do with it
Joey resisted upgrading mics for years. When he finally did, things improved — but only because he'd developed enough understanding to use them properly. The U47 didn't fix his recordings. His knowledge did.
Technical vocabulary is how you influence a session
Joey describes early sessions where he knew something was wrong but couldn't communicate it without the engineer dismissing him. Knowing the language isn't about sounding smart — it's how you shape the outcome of a session instead of just executing someone else's decisions.
Focused study beats scattered effort — always
20 hours to get 75% of the way there. Now he gets further in 2. Joey's pandemic experience is a case study in what happens when you stop copying by feel and start building real understanding. The ROI of genuine study is enormous.
Reflection Questions

These aren't quiz questions. They're worth sitting with honestly — especially if you've been recording for a while and still feel like you're guessing.

→ Can you explain why your overhead placement sounds the way it does — or are you placing them where you always have?
→ When something sounds off in your recording, what's your actual troubleshooting process? Do you have one — or do you move knobs until it improves?
→ How much time have you spent on a single track trying to get a sound that still didn't fully satisfy you? What was actually missing?
→ Do you understand how phase relationships between your microphones affect your drum sound — and can you hear it when something's wrong?
→ If a producer asked you to dial in a specific sound on the spot — something you'd heard before but hadn't recorded — could you get there? What would stop you?
